Volumetric printing beyond viscous resins

What my doctoral work is about, and how my earlier projects connect to it.

Volumetric additive manufacturing (VAM) builds a part in one step. Computed light patterns are projected into a rotating vial of photopolymer, and the part cures everywhere at once, in seconds and without supports. It works best with clear, viscous resins: opaque or filled resins scatter the light, and in thin resins the curing part drifts. It also prints one material at a time.

My doctoral research at Tarbiat Modares University works on those limits. With colleagues in Dr. Behravesh's group I introduced hybrid injection–volumetric additive manufacturing (HIVAM), which pairs directional volumetric curing with synchronised in-situ injection of a second material. Opaque, filled and low-viscosity resins, multi-material parts and encapsulated reinforcement become printable.

Questions I am working on

  1. RQ1How can opaque, filled and low-viscosity resins be cured volumetrically while the part stays fixed in the vial?
  2. RQ2How do projection filtering and dose distribution control the geometric accuracy of the cured part?
  3. RQ3How can fibres and inserts be encapsulated during volumetric curing, and what do they add mechanically?
BAboutSheet 3 / 8

Process first, then the part

I am a Ph.D. researcher in mechanical engineering at Tarbiat Modares University, supervised by Dr. Amirhossein Behravesh. I build or adapt the printing process, make the specimens, and measure what they can carry.

My master's thesis produced a surgical mesh by direct ink writing, with continuous PGA fibre laid into a PCL matrix during printing. I have also worked on hybrid injection–volumetric printing, pellet-fed screw extrusion of PEEK, and 3D-printed lattices as reinforcement for concrete.

My background is in automotive mechanics, and I came to research through hands-on work on engines, machines and test rigs. That is why my projects end with a test, not a render.

M.Sc. thesis · 2023–2026Manuscript submitted

Surgical mesh reinforced with continuous PGA fibre by direct ink writing

A biodegradable hernia mesh printed by direct ink writing. Continuous polyglycolic acid suture fibre is laid into the centre of every PCL strand during printing, and a PVA coating carrying ZnO nanoparticles, added for antibacterial potential, is written on with the same machine. I characterised ink rheology, chemistry, wettability, tensile and burst strength, and cytocompatibility with L929 fibroblasts.
