AProblemSheet 2 / 5

Fast, but picky about resin

Volumetric additive manufacturing (VAM) projects a sequence of computed light patterns into a rotating vial of photopolymer, and the whole part cures at once in seconds, without supports or layers.

That speed comes with strict material rules. The resin must be highly transparent so light reaches the whole volume, which rules out opaque resins and resins carrying light-scattering fillers. Low-viscosity resins are difficult because the partly cured part moves before it is finished. And a conventional VAM print contains one material.

Opaque resins
Block light from reaching the far side of the vial
Filled resins
Scatter light, blurring the projected dose
Thin resins
Let the curing part drift or sink
Single material
No inserts, cavities or second phase in one print
BApproachSheet 3 / 5

Cure the shell, inject the core

HIVAM combines two actions in one print. Directional volumetric curing builds the part from a printable resin, and a needle injects a second material into the vial in sync with the cure. The injected material can fill a cavity, form a reinforced core, or remain liquid inside a cured shell.

The study compared several injection algorithms and processing conditions to find stable fabrication windows, where the injected material stays in place and the part keeps its dimensions.
